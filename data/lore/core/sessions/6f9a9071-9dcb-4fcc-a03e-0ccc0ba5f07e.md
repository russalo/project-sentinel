The road to Hallowrun narrows as the last light bleeds from the sky. You count seven chimneys in the village square, though the map in your pocket names six. The river glints like broken glass where it should be black. At the inn’s threshold, the air thickens—a pressure that makes your teeth ache. Mir Halder’s hands, when they set your mug on the table, do not pass through the wood. The firelight shows her face only in fragments: a scar like a misplaced parenthesis, the hollow where a pupil should be. She says, “Three pennies for the straw mattress. The dreams are free,” and you notice the bell rope is missing from the church steeple outside, though the bell itself is there, rusted shut.  

Upstairs, the Pell family’s laughter rises from the mill, syncopated, as if each syllable is borrowed and must be returned. Their millstones grind something that smells of iron and old names. The inn’s floorboards creak when no one treads them. You find a letter on your pillow, sealed with wax the color of a fresh bruise. The handwriting is yours.
